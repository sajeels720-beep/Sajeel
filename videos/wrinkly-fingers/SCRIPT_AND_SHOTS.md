# "Why Do Your Fingers Wrinkle In Water?" — script and shot plan

A 25-second Short (750 frames at 30 fps), shorter than the first two as you asked. Same format: second person, a hook, a quick twist, the **"You see,"** explanation, a **"But…"** turn, and a callback to the opening. The edit will follow your voiceover recording, as before.

## The new character

Not the brain-freeze guy or the sneeze heroine. A young man in his early twenties, built with MPFB / MakeHuman (all CC0):

- East Asian features, light warm skin, dark brown eyes, thick straight brows;
- short black hair, wet and glossy from the pool;
- a **red swim shirt** (a fitted rash guard) with **Jay3Dfilms** in off-white across the chest. Red pops against the turquoise pool and the blue backdrop, and it's a different colour from video 1's yellow and video 2's coral. Navy swim shorts, mostly under water;
- big, readable acting: blissful eyes-closed lounging, a baffled squint at his raisin fingers, a proud grin.

The story is set in a pool rather than a bath so he can wear the Jay3Dfilms shirt.

**Science check:**
- **It's the nerves.** In the 1930s, doctors (Lewis and Pickering) noticed that fingers whose nerve had been cut didn't wrinkle in water, and doctors have used the soak test to check the nerves in the hand ever since (Wilder-Smith, *Clinical Autonomic Research*, 2004).
- **How it works.** As fingers wrinkle, the blood flow in the fingertips drops because the small vessels constrict (Wilder-Smith and Chow, *Muscle & Nerve*, 2003). Water getting into the sweat pores is thought to set it off; the fleshy pad loses a little volume and the skin on top buckles into wrinkles (Wilder-Smith 2004; Davis, *PLOS ONE*, 2021). The outer skin also swells slightly, which is why the script says "not just the water".
- **Why: grip.** The "rain tread" idea comes from Changizi and colleagues (*Brain, Behavior and Evolution*, 2011). In tests, people with wrinkly fingers moved underwater marbles about 12% faster (Kareklas, Nettle and Smulders, *Biology Letters*, 2013) and needed less force to hold wet objects (Davis 2021). One study found no benefit (Haseleu and colleagues, *PLOS ONE*, 2014), so the script says "Scientists think".
